Research and sources · 5 November 2026

Portugal has been talking about bad smells for years — What happened next?

People complained. Parliament made recommendations. Researchers tested ways to map odours. The Government discussed new rules. Yet residents are still asking a simple question: who will actually investigate the smells affecting their homes?

A promise is not the same as a solution

Portugal has not ignored the subject entirely. The striking part of this story is that many of the ideas residents need today were already on the table years ago. What remains unclear is how fully they were put into practice and what difference they made in everyday life.

2017 · Arrifana, Santa Maria da Feira

People complained — Parliament intervened

In Arrifana, complaints about environmental problems and unpleasant odours reached the national Parliament. Parliament adopted Resolution 190/2017, recommending urgent action.

That sounds like progress — but a recommendation is not proof that the smell stopped. What investigations followed, what changed on the ground, and did residents get lasting relief?

2019–2021 · São João da Madeira

What if residents could put smells on a map?

The European D-NOSES project, with Portuguese participation, explored a different approach: listening to people where the smells were experienced, recording observations and bringing communities, specialists and authorities into the conversation.

It showed that residents' experiences can be collected systematically. But a pilot project is not the same as a permanent public service. Where can someone in Azurva, Aradas or Cacia turn today to make sure such information leads to a coordinated investigation?

2020 · A national recommendation

Parliament called for limits and monitoring of odours

In Resolution 53/2020, the Portuguese Parliament recommended that the Government define ways to measure and monitor nuisance odours, establish legal concentration limits, identify the most affected areas and include odour assessment in annual reporting.

These are not vague hopes. They are recognisable steps toward a system in which residents would not have to prove everything alone.

But this resolution is a recommendation, not a law imposing those limits. Which measures were actually introduced? Which were not — and why?

2021 · A law was being developed

The Government also discussed legislation

In a parliamentary response, the Ministry of the Environment stated that legislation was being developed to prevent and control odours from certain activities, with the aim of protecting people's health and well-being. The 2021 budget explanatory material also referred to approving an odour-related legal instrument.

Was that instrument adopted? If so, what does it cover? If not, what prevented it? We have not yet verified a definitive legislative outcome, so this remains a question rather than an accusation.

2024 · A revealing clarification

And then APA explained an important gap

In January 2024, the Portuguese Environment Agency explained that odours were not monitored through the national air-quality monitoring network. Certain activities still have specific legal obligations; this is not a claim that odours are entirely outside environmental law.

But for a resident who regularly smells something unbearable, the practical question remains: who investigates a recurring nuisance when its source is unknown?

2026 · Azurva and Aveiro

Six years later, I am still asking for answers

Since July 2026, I have been documenting repeated odour episodes, writing to public authorities and asking for an investigation. Some checks and replies have been reported. Yet I still have not been told conclusively where the recurring odours come from or what will be done to reduce them.

Other residents have shared experiences from different places. These reports are not proof of a common source — and that is precisely the point. Investigating potential sources is a technical and institutional responsibility, not something an individual resident can do from a balcony.

So what happened to the promises?

We are not claiming that nothing was done. Some initiatives happened, and different activities are already subject to environmental rules. What we still need is a transparent account of which recommendations were implemented, whether the proposed legislation progressed, and how an ordinary person can obtain a meaningful investigation today.

Clean air should not depend on someone's ability to become a full-time investigator.

Sources are linked beside each documented initiative. Parliamentary recommendations, projects, draft plans and binding law are different things. Outcomes and implementation remain subject to further verification.
